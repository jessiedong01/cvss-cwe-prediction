\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Our CWE evaluation using descriptions alone is likely to underestimate achievable performance, since CWE prediction is much easier when, unlike us but like NVD analysts, you can draw on context outside the description (e.g., by reading the original advisory).
This point bears out in the CWEs predicted incorrectly according to the NVD in Table~\ref{tab:examples}, where the CVSS vectors are nonetheless correct.
(More CWE disagreements between the model and the NVD appear in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.)
For CVE-2024-0204 (an authentication bypass in GoAnywhere MFT), the NVD chooses CWE-425 (direct request, also called forced browsing), while our model chooses CWE-287 (improper authentication), arguably a more intuitive CWE.
For CVE-2024-0149 (a vulnerability in an NVIDIA driver allowing ``unauthorized access to files'' and a ``limited information disclosure''), the NVD chooses CWE-125 (out-of-bounds read), while our model chooses CWE-200 (exposure of sensitive information); it's true that the information is sensitive, but the description makes no mention of an out-of-bounds read.
Some fraction of the CWE errors reported in Table~\ref{tab:main} stem from such disagreements between the text and the NVD's CWE labels, and several of the other errors in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} are alternative interpretations a human might endorse.

Our benchmark evaluates each model on a single (held out) year's worth of test CVEs.
Moreover, each model is trained just once.
Accordingly, the error bars reported in Appendix~\ref{app:results} do not reflect variability from training, but only from randomly sampling test-year CVEs.

For space reasons, we only report the performance of our zero-shot LLM on a random sample of \nSub{} test CVEs, not on the entire year as for other models.
As such, the confidence intervals reported in Table~\ref{tab:subset} do not reflect the size of our full test set, but rather the \nSub{}-CVE subset.
