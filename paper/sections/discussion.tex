\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

In our evaluation, a fine-tuned small language model recovers NVD enrichment from text more accurately than classical baselines, but by less than the difference in model size would suggest.
We examine where the two trained systems disagree, what their errors look like on individual CVEs, and what this implies for deploying either system, and we outline directions for future work.

\paragraph{The two trained systems make complementary errors.}
A difference of a few points in accuracy says little about whether two systems make the same mistakes, and on this test set they do not.
Both systems label the severity correctly on \nBothSev\% of CVEs and both fail on \nNeitherSev\%.
On the remaining \nLoRAOnlySev\% only the fine-tuned model is correct, and on \nLROnlySev\% only TF-IDF is.
One or the other is therefore correct on \nEitherSev\% of CVEs, well above the \nLoRAfSev\% of the better system alone.
The pattern repeats for the CWE (either correct on \nEitherCwe\% against \nLoRAfCwe\%) and for the exact vector (\nEitherVec\% against \nLoRAfVec\%).
We did not build an ensemble, so these figures are an upper bound rather than a result, but they indicate that the two systems read descriptions differently and that a combination is worth pursuing.

\paragraph{Most vector misses are single-component misses, and the missing information is often absent from the text.}
The exact-vector rate is a severe metric, since one wrong component out of eight counts as a miss, and most misses are small.
The fine-tuned model gets every component right on \nLoRAfVecZero\% of test CVEs, exactly one wrong on \nLoRAfVecOne\%, two wrong on \nLoRAfVecTwo\%, and three or more wrong on \nLoRAfVecThreePlus\%, and TF-IDF has the same shape (\nLRfVecZero\%, \nLRfVecOne\%, \nLRfVecTwo\%, and \nLRfVecThreePlus\%).
When exactly one component is wrong, it is privileges required \nLoRAfSolePR\% of the time for the fine-tuned model and \nLRfSolePR\% for TF-IDF, with availability impact a distant second at \nLoRAfSoleAvail\% and \nLRfSoleAvail\%.

Table~\ref{tab:examples} shows what this looks like on individual CVEs.
CVE-2024-0003, our running example, is a privileges-required miss.
The description says a ``malicious user'' can create an account through ``a remote administrative service.''
NIST read ``administrative'' as meaning the attacker already holds high privileges and scored the CVE \nExPurityTrue{}, whereas the model read ``malicious user'' as an outsider, set privileges required to none, and produced \nExPurityPred{} with every other component matching.
A single ambiguous word turns a \textsc{High} into a \textsc{Critical}, which is why this one component accounts for so much of the gap between partial-match and exact-match accuracy.
CVE-2024-0008 is a user-interaction miss of the same kind: PAN-OS management sessions that do not expire are scored \nExPanOSTrue{} by NVD with user interaction required, and the model, seeing no mention of a user, scored them \nExPanOSPred{}.
In both cases the model's reading is defensible from the text, and the information it lacks is not in the description.

\paragraph{The Low tier fails for a reason beyond its rarity.}
Table~\ref{tab:tiermae} gives score MAE by true tier.
The fine-tuned model's score is off by \nLoRAfMaeCritical{} on average for \textsc{Critical} CVEs, by \nLoRAfMaeHigh{} for \textsc{High}, by \nLoRAfMaeMedium{} for \textsc{Medium}, and by \nLoRAfMaeLow{} for \textsc{Low}, and ridge regression, which never commits to a vector, does no better on \textsc{Low} at \nLRdMaeLow{}.
A \textsc{Low} CVE that the model gets wrong is usually wrong by more than one tier.

The examples show why.
CVE-2024-0257 describes a heap-based buffer overflow in RoboDK that ``may crash the application.''
NVD scored it \nExRoboDKTrue{} with limited impact, because the attack needs a crafted project file and the worst outcome mentioned is a crash, while the model scored it \nExRoboDKPred{} with high impact on all three dimensions, the typical score for a heap overflow in the training data.
It applied the prior for the weakness type and ignored the limiting clause.
CVE-2024-10214, a Mattermost bug that issues two sessions during desktop single sign-on, is scored \nExMattermostTrue{} by NVD and \nExMattermostPred{} by the model, which read a session-handling flaw as an authentication bypass.
Both errors run in the same direction: when a description contains words associated with serious weaknesses, the model scores the weakness rather than the sentence.
Class-balanced weights help TF-IDF on this tier, and we did not try the equivalent for the fine-tuned model, which would be oversampling \textsc{Low} during training.

\paragraph{Both systems degrade on short descriptions, and fine-tuning does not rescue them.}
Table~\ref{tab:length} splits the test set into quartiles of description length.
Both systems do worst on the shortest quarter, under \nLenBB{} characters, where the fine-tuned model reaches \nLoRAfSevQA\% severity accuracy and TF-IDF \nLRfSevQA\%.
Accuracy rises through the middle quartiles and levels off, with the fine-tuned model at \nLoRAfSevQC\% on the third quartile and \nLoRAfSevQD\% on the longest, and the exact-vector rate follows the same curve.
The shortest descriptions are often a product name and a weakness class with nothing else, as in CVE-2024-0057 (``.NET, .NET Framework, and Visual Studio Security Feature Bypass Vulnerability''), and for those the score must come from the prior for that vendor and weakness.
The fine-tuned model's lead over TF-IDF is similar in every quartile, so the additional capacity does not specifically help short inputs.

\paragraph{Some CWE errors are disagreements with the analyst, not with the text.}
Two of the examples in Table~\ref{tab:examples} have an exact vector and a wrong CWE.
For CVE-2024-0204, an authentication bypass in GoAnywhere MFT, NVD assigned CWE-425 (direct request) and the model CWE-287 (improper authentication).
For CVE-2024-0149, an NVIDIA driver flaw that allows ``unauthorized access to files'' leading to ``limited information disclosure,'' NVD assigned CWE-125 (out-of-bounds read) and the model CWE-200 (exposure of sensitive information), although the description never mentions a bounds violation.
The NVD label records what the analyst learned from the advisory, and the model's label records what the description says.
Some share of the CWE errors in Table~\ref{tab:main} are of this kind, and the CWE accuracy of any description-only system is bounded by it.

\paragraph{Deployment.}
The results point to a simple division of labor.
TF-IDF logistic regression trains in minutes, needs no GPU, and gives up a few points on every field, and it should be used with formula scoring, which costs nothing and repairs the rare tiers, unless precision on \textsc{Critical} matters more than recall.
The fine-tuned model is better on every field, produces consistent scores and vectors, and can name CWEs outside the frequent set, at a cost of \nLoraTrainHours{} hours of training and about half a second per CVE at inference, which is fast enough for the daily volume of new disclosures.
The zero-shot model should not be used for this task.
Whichever system is deployed, the \textsc{Low} tier and the privileges-required component should be treated as unreliable, and a CVE scored 9.8 by either trained system deserves review before it moves to the front of a queue.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Several extensions follow directly from these findings.
Oversampling \textsc{Low} or weighting its loss should be tried for the fine-tuned model, since the same change helped TF-IDF.
A combination of the two trained systems, by agreement or by confidence, would test whether the \nEitherSev\% upper bound can be approached.
A second training epoch is cheap, and the validation curve suggests it would help.
Larger open models fine-tuned the same way, and an evaluation on CVSS v4.0 vectors, would show whether these results hold beyond the setting we study.
