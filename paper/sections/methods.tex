\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Direct and Formula Scoring}
Every system predicts the eight vector components and the CWE.
We then produce the score and severity in one of two ways.
Under \emph{direct} scoring, a separate model predicts the score by regression and the severity by classification, or a language model writes both itself.
Under \emph{formula} scoring, we apply the CVSS v3.1 equations to the predicted components, so that score, severity, and vector agree by construction.
The TF-IDF systems share one ridge regression for the direct score, so their direct score errors are identical and we report them once.

\subsection{TF-IDF Baselines}
We represent each description with TF-IDF over unigrams and bigrams, with sublinear term frequency, a minimum document frequency of 2, and at most 50,000 features.
We keep stop words, because ``not'' and ``without'' change the meaning of a description.
We train one classifier per output with scikit-learn~\citep{pedregosa2011sklearn}.
Logistic regression (LR) chooses its regularization strength $C \in \{0.3, 1, 3, 10\}$ by validation accuracy, separately for severity, for CWE, and for the components, with one $C$ shared by all eight components.
Class-balanced LR uses the same setup with class weights inversely proportional to class frequency.
We also include an untuned random forest (RF) with 300 trees for severity and CWE and 100 trees per component.
The direct score comes from ridge regression with $\alpha \in \{0.3, 1, 3, 10\}$ chosen by validation MAE.
The CWE classifiers learn the 100 most frequent training CWEs plus an \textsc{Other} class, and we score them against the true CWE, so an \textsc{Other} prediction always counts as an error.

\subsection{Phi-3.5-mini}
Our language model is Phi-3.5-mini-instruct, a 3.8B-parameter decoder~\citep{abdin2024phi3} whose training data ends in October 2023, before the test period.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning.}
We fine-tune with LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} in MLX~\citep{hannun2023mlx} on a single Apple M5 Pro with 64\,GB of unified memory.
Adapters of rank 16 (scale 20, dropout 0.05) sit on the last 16 transformer layers and amount to \nLoraParams{}M trainable parameters, or \nLoraPct\% of the model.
We train for 5,000 steps at batch size 8, one pass over the training set, with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$, 100 warm-up steps, and cosine decay to $10^{-5}$, and the loss covers only the answer tokens.
We drop the \nDropped{} training examples (\nDroppedPct\%) longer than 768 tokens to bound memory and keep every test CVE regardless of length.
The target is a single JSON object that lists the eight components first and then the CWE, the score, and the severity, so the model commits to a vector before it writes a score.
We evaluate checkpoints at steps 1,000 through 5,000 on the same 1,000 validation CVEs (Table~\ref{tab:ckpt}) and keep the one with the best severity accuracy under formula scoring.
Decoding is greedy.

\paragraph{Zero-shot.}
The same base model, without an adapter, receives the description and a prompt that lists every field and its allowed values (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).
Zero-shot output is verbose and slow to generate for a whole year of CVEs, so we evaluate it on a random sample of \nSub{} test CVEs and rescore every other system on the same sample.

\subsection{Metrics}
We evaluate severity with accuracy and with macro-F1 over the four tiers.
Macro-F1 weights \textsc{Low} as heavily as \textsc{Medium} and so exposes failures on the rare tiers that accuracy hides.
We evaluate CWE prediction with top-1 accuracy, the score with mean absolute error (MAE), and the vector with per-component accuracy, the mean fraction of components correct (\emph{partial match}), and the fraction of CVEs with all eight components correct (\emph{exact match}).
A language-model output that is not valid JSON counts as wrong on every field and is excluded from the score MAE, and we report the parse rate separately.
All 95\% confidence intervals come from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of test CVEs.
