\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We synthesize the main findings from our evaluation, with results summarized in Table~\ref{tab:main} and Table~\ref{tab:tiers}.

\input{tables/main}

\paragraph{Fine-tuning a small language model outperforms TF-IDF on every field, by a modest margin.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports every system on the \nTest{} test CVEs.
The fine-tuned Phi-3.5 model leads on every metric, reaching \nLoRAfSev\% severity accuracy, \nLoRAfCwe\% CWE accuracy, a score MAE of \nLoRAfMae{}, and an exact vector on \nLoRAfVec\% of CVEs.
Because every system is scored on the same CVEs, we compare them with a paired bootstrap against TF-IDF logistic regression with formula scoring.
Relative to that baseline, the fine-tuned model improves severity accuracy by \nDiffLRfSev{} points (95\% CI \nDiffLRfSevLo{} to \nDiffLRfSevHi{}), macro-F1 by \nDiffLRfFone{} (\nDiffLRfFoneLo{} to \nDiffLRfFoneHi{}), CWE accuracy by \nDiffLRfCwe{} (\nDiffLRfCweLo{} to \nDiffLRfCweHi{}), score MAE by \nDiffLRfMae{} (\nDiffLRfMaeLo{} to \nDiffLRfMaeHi{}), and the exact-vector rate by \nDiffLRfVec{} (\nDiffLRfVecLo{} to \nDiffLRfVecHi{}).
No interval includes zero.
The gains are consistent, but they are a few points, and TF-IDF remains a strong baseline that any method for this task should be required to beat.

\paragraph{Computing the score from the predicted vector helps the linear model and trades precision for recall on the Critical tier.}
For logistic regression, formula scoring lowers score MAE from \nLRdMae{} to \nLRfMae{} and raises macro-F1 from \nLRdFone\% to \nLRfFone\% while accuracy stays at \nLRfSev\%.
The gain comes from the two rare tiers (Table~\ref{tab:tiers}): \textsc{Low} F1 rises from \nLRdFoneLow\% to \nLRfFoneLow\%, and \textsc{Critical} F1 from \nLRdFoneCritical\% to \nLRfFoneCritical\% as \textsc{Critical} recall climbs from \nLRdRecCritical\% to \nLRfRecCritical\%.
The improvement has a cost.
The formula system predicts \textsc{Critical} for \nLRfCritPred{} CVEs where NVD has \nLRfCritTrue{}, and its precision on that tier falls from \nLRdCritPrec\% to \nLRfCritPrec\%.
It scores \nLRfNineEight\% of test CVEs at exactly 9.8, the score of the most common critical vector, against \nNvdNineEight\% in NVD, and its mean signed score error is \nLRfBias{} compared with \nLRdBias{} for ridge regression.
For the random forest, whose component predictions are weaker, formula scoring passes those errors through to the score: accuracy falls from \nRFdSev\% to \nRFfSev\% and MAE rises from \nRFdMae{} to \nRFfMae{}.

\paragraph{The fine-tuned model learns to apply the formula itself.}
The fine-tuned model shows almost no difference between the two scoring modes.
The score it writes matches the CVSS formula applied to its own vector on \nLoRAScoreCons\% of test CVEs, and the severity it writes matches the tier of its own score on \nLoRASevCons\% (Table~\ref{tab:consistency}).
Trained on targets that list the components before the score, the model behaves as if it computes the score from the vector it has just written.

\paragraph{Errors concentrate in the Low tier and in the privileges-required component.}
\textsc{Low} is the hardest tier for every system (Table~\ref{tab:tiers}).
It makes up under 2\% of the training data, and both trained systems most often label a true \textsc{Low} CVE as \textsc{Medium}.
Class-balanced weights raise \textsc{Low} recall to \nLRBfRecLow\% for TF-IDF, and the fine-tuned model reaches \nLoRAfRecLow\% without any rebalancing.
Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows that most of the remaining confusion falls between adjacent tiers.
Across components (Figure~\ref{fig:components}), attack complexity, scope, attack vector, and user interaction exceed 90\% for both systems, because descriptions usually state them outright.
Privileges required is the hardest component, at \nLRfCompPR\% for TF-IDF and \nLoRAfCompPR\% for the fine-tuned model, followed by the three impact components.
The fine-tuned model gains most on confidentiality (\nLRfCompC\% to \nLoRAfCompC\%), integrity (\nLRfCompI\% to \nLoRAfCompI\%), and privileges required, the components that depend least on surface keywords.
\citet{marchiori2025canllms} found that general-purpose LLMs trail embedding-based classifiers on the impact components, and we find that after fine-tuning these are among the components where the language model gains most.

\paragraph{Only the generative model can reach rare CWEs.}
The TF-IDF classifiers can output only the 100 most frequent training CWEs and therefore score 0\% on the \nRareCweN{} test CVEs whose CWE is rarer.
The fine-tuned model writes the CWE as text and identifies \nLoRAfRareCwe\% of them correctly (Figure~\ref{fig:cwe}).
Common, explicitly named weaknesses such as cross-site scripting and SQL injection are close to solved by both systems.

\paragraph{Both trained systems over-predict the most common critical score.}
Ridge regression produces a smooth spread of scores that NVD never assigns and rarely predicts the extremes (Figure~\ref{fig:scores}).
The fine-tuned model's scores lie on the same discrete values as NVD's, but it predicts 9.8 for \nLoRAfNineEight\% of test CVEs where NVD assigns it to \nNvdNineEight\%.
It predicts \textsc{Critical} for \nLoRAfCritPred{} test CVEs against \nLoRAfCritTrue{} in NVD, with \nLoRAfCritPrec\% precision, and like formula-scored logistic regression it runs high on average, with a mean signed error of \nLoRAfBias.

\paragraph{Zero-shot prompting fails on this task, and its outputs contradict themselves.}
\input{tables/subset}
Without fine-tuning, the same base model is far weaker (Table~\ref{tab:subset}).
It returned valid JSON for \nZSParse\% of the \nSub{} sampled CVEs, and \nZSInvalid{} of the parsed outputs used a component value outside the allowed set.
Its written severity is biased upward: it labels \nZSCritShare\% of the sample \textsc{Critical} against \nSubCritShare\% in NVD, predicts \textsc{Medium} only \nZSMediumCount{} times although \nSubMediumShare\% of these CVEs are \textsc{Medium}, and is correct on \nZSdSev\% of CVEs.
The written score matches the formula for the written vector on \nZSScoreCons\% of CVEs, and the written severity matches the tier of the written score on \nZSSevCons\% (Table~\ref{tab:consistency}).
Computing severity from the vector removes the inconsistency and lifts severity accuracy to \nZSfSev\%, still far below the fine-tuned model (\nLoRAfSubSev\%) and TF-IDF (\nLRfSubSev\%) on the same CVEs, while score MAE worsens from \nZSdMae{} to \nZSfMae{} because the zero-shot vectors are wrong in ways that move the score a long way.
The per-component accuracies explain this (Figure~\ref{fig:components}): attack complexity (\nZSCompAC\%) and attack vector (\nZSCompAV\%) approach the trained systems, but availability impact is correct on only \nZSCompA\% of CVEs, integrity on \nZSCompI\%, and confidentiality on \nZSCompC\%.
The zero-shot model reads how an attack happens reasonably well and guesses what it costs.

\paragraph{Training is cheap, and validation accuracy had not plateaued.}
Validation severity accuracy rose at every checkpoint, and we selected the last one at step \nBestStep{} (Table~\ref{tab:ckpt}, Figure~\ref{fig:val}).
Macro-F1 peaked earlier, at step \nBestFoneStep{} with \nBestFoneVal\%, so a deployment that weights the rare tiers more heavily might prefer that checkpoint.
