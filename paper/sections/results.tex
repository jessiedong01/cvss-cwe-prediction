\subsection{Main Results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports every system on the \nTest{} test CVEs.
The fine-tuned Phi-3.5 model leads on every metric: \nLoRAfSev\% severity accuracy, \nLoRAfCwe\% CWE accuracy, a score MAE of \nLoRAfMae{}, and an exact vector on \nLoRAfVec\% of CVEs.
Because every system is scored on the same CVEs, a paired bootstrap gives the difference against TF-IDF logistic regression with formula scoring directly.
The fine-tuned model changes severity accuracy by \nDiffLRfSev{} points (95\% CI \nDiffLRfSevLo{} to \nDiffLRfSevHi{}), macro-F1 by \nDiffLRfFone{} (\nDiffLRfFoneLo{} to \nDiffLRfFoneHi{}), CWE accuracy by \nDiffLRfCwe{} (\nDiffLRfCweLo{} to \nDiffLRfCweHi{}), score MAE by \nDiffLRfMae{} (\nDiffLRfMaeLo{} to \nDiffLRfMaeHi{}), and the exact-vector rate by \nDiffLRfVec{} (\nDiffLRfVecLo{} to \nDiffLRfVecHi{}).
No interval includes zero.
The gains are consistent, but they are a few points, and TF-IDF remains a strong baseline for this task.

\subsection{Effect of Formula Scoring}
For logistic regression, computing the score from the predicted vector lowers score MAE from \nLRdMae{} to \nLRfMae{} and raises macro-F1 from \nLRdFone\% to \nLRfFone\%.
Accuracy stays at \nLRfSev\%.
The gain comes from the two rare tiers (Table~\ref{tab:tiers}).
\textsc{Low} F1 rises from \nLRdFoneLow\% to \nLRfFoneLow\%, and \textsc{Critical} F1 from \nLRdFoneCritical\% to \nLRfFoneCritical\% as \textsc{Critical} recall climbs from \nLRdRecCritical\% to \nLRfRecCritical\%.

The improvement has a cost.
The formula system predicts \textsc{Critical} for \nLRfCritPred{} CVEs where NVD has \nLRfCritTrue{}, and its precision on that tier drops from \nLRdCritPrec\% to \nLRfCritPrec\%.
It scores \nLRfNineEight\% of test CVEs at exactly 9.8, the score of the most common critical vector (network, low complexity, no privileges, no interaction, high impact on all three dimensions), against \nNvdNineEight\% in NVD.
Its mean signed score error is \nLRfBias{}, compared with \nLRdBias{} for ridge regression.
For a triage queue, catching more critical CVEs at the price of more false alarms may be the right trade, but it is a trade.

For the random forest, formula scoring hurts.
Accuracy falls from \nRFdSev\% to \nRFfSev\% and MAE rises from \nRFdMae{} to \nRFfMae{}.
Its component predictions are weaker, and the formula passes their errors straight through to the score.

The fine-tuned model shows almost no difference between the two modes.
The score it writes matches the CVSS formula for its own vector on \nLoRAScoreCons\% of test CVEs, and the severity it writes matches its own score on \nLoRASevCons\% (Table~\ref{tab:consistency}).
Trained on targets that list the components first, the model behaves as if it applies the formula to its own vector.

\subsection{Error Analysis}
\paragraph{Severity tiers.}
\textsc{Low} is the hardest tier for every system (Table~\ref{tab:tiers}).
It makes up under 2\% of the training data, and both systems most often label a true \textsc{Low} CVE as \textsc{Medium}.
Class-balanced weights raise \textsc{Low} recall to \nLRBfRecLow\% for TF-IDF.
The fine-tuned model reaches \nLoRAfRecLow\% with no rebalancing at all.
Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows that most of the remaining confusion is between adjacent tiers.

\paragraph{CVSS components.}
Figure~\ref{fig:components} gives accuracy per component.
Attack complexity, scope, attack vector, and user interaction are above 90\% for both systems, because descriptions usually state them outright.
Privileges required is the hardest component, at \nLRfCompPR\% for TF-IDF and \nLoRAfCompPR\% for the fine-tuned model, followed by the three impact components.
The fine-tuned model gains most on confidentiality (\nLRfCompC\% to \nLoRAfCompC\%), integrity (\nLRfCompI\% to \nLoRAfCompI\%), and privileges required, the components that depend least on surface keywords.
\citet{marchiori2025canllms} found that general-purpose LLMs trail embedding-based classifiers on the impact components.
After fine-tuning, those are among the components where the model gains most.

\centering

\label{fig:components}

\paragraph{Rare CWEs.}
The TF-IDF classifiers can only output the 100 most frequent training CWEs, so they score 0\% on the \nRareCweN{} test CVEs whose CWE is rarer.
The fine-tuned model writes the CWE as text and gets \nLoRAfRareCwe\% of them right (Figure~\ref{fig:cwe}).
Common, explicitly named weaknesses such as cross-site scripting and SQL injection are close to solved by both systems.

\paragraph{Score distribution.}
Ridge regression produces a smooth spread of scores that NVD never assigns, and it rarely predicts the extremes (Figure~\ref{fig:scores}).
The fine-tuned model's scores sit on the same discrete values as NVD's, but it predicts the most common critical score, 9.8, for \nLoRAfNineEight\% of test CVEs where NVD assigns it to \nNvdNineEight\%.
It predicts \textsc{Critical} for \nLoRAfCritPred{} test CVEs against \nLoRAfCritTrue{} in NVD, with \nLoRAfCritPrec\% precision, and like formula-scored logistic regression it runs high on average, with a mean signed error of \nLoRAfBias.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Baseline}
\input{tables/subset}
Without fine-tuning, the same base model is far weaker (Table~\ref{tab:subset}).
It returned valid JSON for \nZSParse\% of the \nSub{} sampled CVEs, and \nZSInvalid{} of the parsed outputs used a component value outside the allowed set.
The severity it writes is biased upward.
It labels \nZSCritShare\% of the sample \textsc{Critical}, against \nSubCritShare\% in NVD, and predicts \textsc{Medium} only \nZSMediumCount{} times although \nSubMediumShare\% of these CVEs are \textsc{Medium}.
Its written severity is correct on \nZSdSev\% of CVEs.
The outputs also contradict themselves.
The written score matches the formula for the written vector on \nZSScoreCons\% of CVEs, and the written severity matches the tier of the written score on \nZSSevCons\% (Table~\ref{tab:consistency}).
Computing severity from the vector removes the inconsistency and lifts severity accuracy to \nZSfSev\%, still far below the fine-tuned model (\nLoRAfSubSev\%) and TF-IDF (\nLRfSubSev\%) on the same CVEs.
Score MAE gets worse, from \nZSdMae{} to \nZSfMae{}, because the zero-shot vectors are wrong in ways that move the score a long way.
The per-component picture explains this (Figure~\ref{fig:components}).
Attack complexity (\nZSCompAC\%) and attack vector (\nZSCompAV\%) are close to the trained systems, but availability impact is right on only \nZSCompA\% of CVEs, integrity on \nZSCompI\%, and confidentiality on \nZSCompC\%.
The zero-shot model reads how an attack happens reasonably well and guesses what it costs.
