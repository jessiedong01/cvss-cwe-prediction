\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

In the previous sections, we have already observed a couple of strengths and weaknesses in the predictions our methods make.
We further inspect common mistakes and compare our results.

\paragraph{Common Mistakes in CVSS Prediction.}
As can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:components} and Tables~\ref{tab:qualsomea} and~\ref{tab:qualsomeb}, certain CVSS vector components are much easier to predict than others, no matter the method used for prediction.
Attack complexity and attack vector can be predicted with an accuracy of \nZSCompAV\% to \nZSCompAC\% zero-shot, whereas impact to availability, integrity and confidentiality are much more difficult to predict correctly (\nZSCompA\% to \nZSCompC\% zero-shot).

Furthermore, it becomes apparent from the tables that models tend to predict a CVSS vector for the weakness type mentioned in the CVE description if little further information is available or if the limiting factors are not mentioned or difficult to extract.

\paragraph{Training and Run Times.}
We find that severity classification accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:ckpt}) increases per checkpoint.
We validated on 1,000 CVEs from the validation split for each checkpoint (every 1,000 steps, up to 5,000 training steps).
While accuracy increases up to step \nBestStep, Macro-F$_1$ scores peak earlier (optimum at \nBestFoneStep{} steps with a score of \nBestFoneVal\%).
Depending on whether mispredictions of the rarer classes Critical and Low are considered more important to reduce than increasing overall accuracy, the model at step \nBestFoneStep{} might be preferred for deployment.

Fine-tuning takes \nLoraTrainHours{} h, scoring the test set (one year's worth of CVEs) another \nLoraTestHours{} h.
Batches contained at most 32 CVE descriptions.
For comparison, all TF-IDF experiments, including tuning, took only \nTfidfMinutes{} minutes.

Next, we investigate which CVSS vector components models struggle to predict correctly.
Table~\ref{tab:sole} presents the distribution of CVEs in which a given CVSS vector component is predicted incorrectly, for CVEs with one incorrect vector component.
We present this both for our fine-tuned model and for TF-IDF as a representative classical method.
Privileges Required makes up \nLoRAfSolePR\% of such examples for the fine-tuned model and TF-IDF respectively, with Availability Impact making up \nLoRAfSoleAvail\% and \nLRfSoleAvail\% respectively.

Finally, we investigate what causes the difference between CVEs with correct severity versus exact CVSS score match.
To our surprise, most of the gap can be attributed to CVEs with only one incorrect component (see Table~\ref{tab:gap}).
Thus, often a single ambiguous word can determine whether a given vulnerability is Critical or High, for example.
Two examples are presented in Table~\ref{tab:examples}.
In the case of CVE-2024-0003, it appears NIST inferred from the phrasing \emph{administrative service} that this is not easily exploitable by outsiders and gave it a score of \nExPurityTrue{} requiring high privileges.
However, the phrasing \emph{malicious user} led the model to predict an attacker does not need privileges, resulting in a Critical score of \nExPurityPred.
All other vector components were predicted correctly.
In the case of CVE-2024-0008, NVD gave a High score of \nExPanOSTrue{} as User Interaction was deemed required to exploit the vulnerability.
However, no mention is made of any user in the text, leading the model to make an incorrect prediction and give a score of \nExPanOSPred.
Here again, all other components were predicted correctly.
In both cases, one could argue that the model's interpretation is reasonable, given the missing details.

Next we examine MAE of the predicted score by true severity (Table~\ref{tab:tiermae}).
On the 2024 test set, our fine-tuned model's score MAE is \nLoRAfMaeCritical{} for Critical CVEs, \nLoRAfMaeHigh{} on High, \nLoRAfMaeMedium{} on Medium, and \nLoRAfMaeLow{} on Low CVEs.
Low severity CVEs are rare, making accurate prediction challenging for all methods.
Interestingly, Ridge without vector commitment achieves a score MAE of \nLRdMaeLow{} on Low CVEs.
When our model errs on a Low CVE it is usually by more than a severity tier.
Two examples are given in Table~\ref{tab:examples}.
In both cases, the descriptions contain words associated with particularly insidious weakness types.
The first CVE describes a heap overflow, a weakness type our model has likely seen associated with high severity during training, and makes a prediction accordingly.
However, the last sentence mitigates the severity, and NVD scored this accordingly.
The second CVE also vaguely alludes to a common weakness our model likely associates with high severity, where session-handling code can easily be construed as an authentication bypass vulnerability.
More information would be needed to clarify that this is likely less serious.
Thus, when the description alludes to a weakness that our model associates with serious vulnerabilities, but otherwise offers no relevant detail, the model appears to predict according to the weakness, rather than the sentence.
Low severity performs better for TF-IDF with class-balanced weights.
The analogue in a language model would be oversampling Low during training, which we did not explore.

We next analyze model performance as a function of the length of the CVE Description, split into quartiles 1\ldots4 from shortest to longest (with bounds \nLenBB, \nLenBC, and \nLenBD{} on the 2024 test set).
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:length}, model performance is lower when there is less text to work with (severity accuracy on the shortest CVEs being \nLoRAfSevQA\% and \nLRfSevQA\% for our fine-tuned model and TF-IDF respectively), with improvement and leveling off as description length increases (\nLoRAfSevQC\% on the third quartile of lengths and \nLoRAfSevQD\% on the fourth for our model, with a similar trend for exact vector).
Exact vector match results show the same trends as severity.
Many of the shortest descriptions are simply of the form PRODUCTNAME + weakness type, e.g.\ CVE-2024-0057 is titled \emph{.NET, .NET Framework, and Visual Studio Security Feature Bypass Vulnerability}, and the predicted score would be entirely dependent on the learned associations of Microsoft and SFBO vulnerabilities.
While the extra capacity of the larger fine-tuned model may be helpful overall, it has a similar advantage over TF-IDF in all quartiles, and thus does not appear to overcome poor performance on very short CVE descriptions.
