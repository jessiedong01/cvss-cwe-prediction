\section{Introduction}

A disclosed vulnerability gets a CVE identifier and a short description.
The description says what is wrong.
It does not say how urgent the problem is, and a team with a queue of a few hundred CVEs needs to know that first.
For this they use structured \emph{enrichment}: a CVSS v3.1 vector recording how the flaw is exploited and what it affects, the base score (0--10) and severity tier that follow from the vector, and a CWE category for the type of weakness~\citep{first2019cvss31}.
Scanners, patch managers, and incident responders sort by these fields.

For most CVEs the enrichment came from the National Vulnerability Database (NVD) at NIST.
The NVD fell behind in early 2024, and on 15 April 2026 NIST announced that it would from then on enrich only CVEs in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, CVEs in software the federal government uses, and critical software under Executive Order 14028.
Everything else is marked ``Lowest Priority -- not scheduled for immediate enrichment''~\citep{nist2026nvd}.
So for a typical new CVE, a defender now has the description and whatever score the reporting organization chose to attach.

This paper asks how much of the missing enrichment a model can recover from the description alone.
The task, called \textbf{CVE enrichment} here, is to predict the eight CVSS v3.1 vector components, the base score, the severity tier, and the CWE (Figure~\ref{fig:task}).
Three kinds of system are compared on it: TF-IDF classifiers, a small instruction-tuned language model used zero-shot, and the same model fine-tuned with LoRA.

Two choices shape the evaluation.
The first is a split by time.
Training uses CVEs from 2022 and 2023 and testing uses every usable CVE from 2024, so no model is scored on a period it has seen.
A random split would let near-duplicate descriptions of the same product, written weeks apart, land on both sides, and several public models are trained that way.
The second choice is to compute the score rather than predict it.
The CVSS base score and severity are fixed functions of the eight components, so a model can predict the components and apply the formula, as \citet{elbaz2020fighting} and \citet{shahid2021cvssbert} did.
This makes score, severity, and vector consistent by construction.
The paper measures what the computation adds over predicting the score directly, and whether a language model's own score agrees with the vector it writes.

On the full test year the fine-tuned model is the strongest system on every field (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}).
It reaches \nLoRAfSev\% severity accuracy and \nLoRAfCwe\% CWE accuracy, predicts the exact CVSS vector for \nLoRAfVec\% of CVEs, and leads TF-IDF logistic regression by \nDiffLRfSev{} to \nDiffLRfVec{} points.
The margin is modest, and the more useful findings are about where the systems fail.
The two systems disagree on a large minority of CVEs, and one or the other is right on \nEitherSev\% of severities.
When the fine-tuned model misses the vector, it usually misses by one component, and that component is usually privileges required.
Both systems do worst on the shortest descriptions and on the \textsc{Low} tier, which barely exists in the training data.
After \nLoraTrainHours{} hours of fine-tuning, the model's written score matches its own vector on \nLoRAScoreCons\% of CVEs.
Used zero-shot, the same model manages \nZSScoreCons\%.
